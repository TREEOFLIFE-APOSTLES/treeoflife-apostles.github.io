The Black Hole Chirp

GW150914, the first gravitational wave ever detected, 14 September 2015.
This is the real signal from both LIGO detectors.

Hanford (H1) and Livingston (L1), whitened and filtered 35–350 Hz, last 0.6 s before the merger. L1 is shifted 7 ms and flipped to line up with H1.

What is a gravitational wave?

Space and time can stretch and squeeze. When heavy things whirl around each other, they send ripples through space itself, like a boat making waves on a pond. These ripples travel at the speed of light.

Why does it chirp?

Two black holes, about 36 and 29 times the mass of our Sun, circled each other about 1.3 billion years ago. Each orbit sent out ripples and stole a little energy, so they spiraled closer and faster. The wave rose from about 35 to about 250 times per second in a fraction of a second: a rising "whoop", a chirp. Then they merged into one black hole of about 62 Suns.

3 Suns into ripples

36 + 29 = 65, but the new black hole weighed only about 62. About 3 Suns' worth of mass turned straight into gravitational-wave energy (E = mc²). For a split second, it gave off more power than all the stars in the whole observable universe put together.

A whisper on Earth

By the time the ripple reached us, it stretched LIGO's 4 km arms by far less than the width of a proton. Both detectors, 3,000 km apart, felt it 7 thousandths of a second apart.

Circles and frequencies

On our Spinning Circles page, each circle turns at its own speed, its own frequency. Here the frequency is not fixed: it slides upward as the orbit tightens. The gravitational wave's frequency is twice the orbit's, because the pair looks the same every half turn. Scientists matched this rising tone against predictions from Einstein's equations to read the masses.
