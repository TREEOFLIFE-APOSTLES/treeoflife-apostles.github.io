What it is

Scatter fine sand on a square metal plate, make the plate vibrate, and the sand dances away from the moving places and gathers on the places that stay still. Those still curves are called nodal lines, and the picture they draw is a Chladni pattern — a song you can see.

On this page the plate is a square of glowing dust. For chosen whole numbers n and m, the still lines are where cos(nπx)cos(mπy) − cos(mπx)cos(nπy) = 0. The sand is gently shaken where the plate would move most, and settles where that value is near zero. If n and m are equal the two terms cancel everywhere, so choose two different numbers.

Ernst Chladni (1756–1827)

Ernst Chladni was a German physicist and musician, often called the father of acoustics. He drew a violin bow across the edge of a sand-covered plate so the plate rang like an instrument, and the sand collected on its still nodal lines. He published his patterns in Entdeckungen über die Theorie des Klanges in 1787 and spent years travelling with his plates, showing audiences — including, famously, Napoleon — that sound has a visible shape. These figures helped turn the study of vibration into modern acoustics and, much later, into the way we think about waves and modes in physics.

From sound back to geometry

After two circles in the Vesica Piscis and nine triangles in the Sri Yantra, here is a third way a pattern appears: not drawn by a compass or a ruler, but revealed by vibration. The plate decides where the lines are; the sand only tells the truth about where it can rest. Different branches, same tree. Soft eyes.
